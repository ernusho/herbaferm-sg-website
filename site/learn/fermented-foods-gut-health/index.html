Fermented foods and gut health: what counts, and what the research shows

Fermented foods are not all probiotics, and not all fermented products are the same. Here is the difference.

By Herbaferm editorial teamMedical reviewer: PendingPublished 2 Nov 2026 (draft)

In short
  • Fermented foods are made through desired microbial growth and conversion of food components.[1]
  • Only some fermented foods contain live microbes at the time of eating, and a fermented food is not automatically a probiotic.[1][2]
  • One 10-week trial found a high fermented-food diet increased microbiome diversity in healthy adults.[3]

What are fermented foods?

The ISAPP consensus definition is "foods made through desired microbial growth and enzymatic conversions of food components".[1] Yoghurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut, tempeh, miso, natto and sourdough bread are all examples.

Are fermented foods the same as probiotics?

No. Some fermented foods contain live microbes when you eat them, and some are heated, filtered or baked after fermenting so the microbes are gone. A probiotic must have a shown benefit for the person taking it.[2] The consensus statement says that a fermented food is not a probiotic unless it meets that bar.[1]

Which fermented foods are easy to find in Singapore?

Yoghurt and kefir, kimchi, tempeh, miso, natto and fermented bean pastes are widely available in supermarkets and Asian groceries. Check labels for live cultures if that matters to you, and for salt and sugar content.

What does the research say?

In a 2021 Stanford trial of healthy adults over ten weeks, a diet high in fermented foods increased microbiome diversity and lowered several markers of inflammation, while the high-fibre group responded more differently from person to person.[3] The study was small and does not show effects on disease, and results may differ in other groups.

Frequently asked questions

What counts as a fermented food?

Foods made through desired microbial growth and conversion of food components, such as yoghurt, kefir, kimchi, tempeh, miso and sourdough.

Are fermented foods probiotics?

Not automatically. Some contain live microbes and some do not, and a probiotic needs a shown benefit for the person taking it.
